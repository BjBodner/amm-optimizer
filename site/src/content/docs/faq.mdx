Two kinds of benchmark numbers appear on this page. The **final test** (FT1) is the frozen configuration run
once on bbob instance ids <Claim id="ids.final" />, which no design decision used. The **in-sample** numbers are development results on ids <Claim id="ids.dev" />, which
were used while AMM was being built (one seed per instance, not held out). Each number says which it is.

## What is AMM?

AMM (Adaptive Multi Mode) is a derivative-free optimizer for black-box functions. You give it a starting
point, a box and an evaluation budget. It spends a small part of the budget on cheap probes that read the
function (for example how smooth it is, whether it separates into one-variable parts), then routes the rest to
a classical algorithm that suits the reading: a (1+1)-CMA-ES, SLSQP, L-BFGS-B, Nelder-Mead, COBYQA,
restarted population CMA-ES and others. How it works is on the [how it works](/how-it-works/) page.

## Why rename from ATM to AMM?

ATM (Adaptive Two Mode) is the name of the original sampler, which mixes a random search with a search along
the last successful direction. The configuration frozen for the paper, `c46`, is a hybrid: the ATM sampler is
one stage among the classical engines listed above, and the routing between them is the main idea. The name AMM
says that. The package is renamed: the import is `amm_optimizer` (distribution `amm-optimizer`), and the class and preset names (`ATMConfig`, `c46`) keep the old name. Without `config=`, `minimize` runs
<Claim id="pkg.default_config" />. Whether `c46` becomes the default configuration is not decided, so AMM is selected with
`config=amm_optimizer.presets.get("c46")`. AMM alone is ambiguous (automated market maker, adaptive mixture
Metropolis), so the site always writes the full name.

## How is it different from CMA-ES, Nevergrad and Optuna?

AMM is not a new search paradigm. It chooses among existing ones. pycma's IPOP and BIPOP CMA-ES are
competitors in the benchmark, and a CMA-ES variant is also one of AMM's own engines. Nevergrad's NGOpt is
itself a meta-optimizer that selects an algorithm from the problem's dimension and budget, so the difference
claimed for AMM is the probes of the function itself, not the idea of choosing. Whether that difference helps is
what the [results](/results/) measure, on one benchmark suite. Optuna's TPE is a sampler built for
hyperparameter search. In the benchmark it ran on a capped slice of the grid only (see
[Reproduce](/reproduce/)). The field also includes SciPy's SLSQP and differential evolution, and HCMA from the COCO
archive.

## Does AMM beat everything?

No. In the final test, AMM has the highest mean share of targets reached, ties included, in
<Claim id="hero.highest" /> of <Claim id="ft1.cells" /> function, dimension and budget cells
(<Claim id="hero.highest_pct" />). It is credibly behind some competitor in <Claim id="ft1.f2_behind" /> cells, and all of
them are listed on the [limits](/limits/) page. In <Claim id="ft1.f3_ahead_all" /> cells it is credibly ahead of every
competitor that ran the cell. "Credibly" is the preregistered rule: a competitor leads by more than 0.02 in target share
with posterior probability above 0.9 over the ten paired runs. These are counts, not significance statements, because
cell-level intervals of this kind were found to be about 25% too narrow.

Over IPOP the lead is <Claim id="ft1.lead_ipop" />, with bootstrap intervals above zero at every budget. The in-sample
comparison uses a different, simpler rule, so its counts are not comparable with those above: AMM ahead of the best
competitor by more than +0.02 in <Claim id="field.ahead" /> cells, level in <Claim id="field.level" /> and behind by more
than 0.02 in <Claim id="field.behind" />, out of <Claim id="hero.cells" /> cells, on one seed per instance. That 0.02
is a plotting threshold, not a significance test.

## What budgets and dimensions does it handle?

It was tested on 24 bbob functions in dimensions 2, 5, 10, 20 and 40, with budgets of 10, 30, 100, 300 and
1000 evaluations per dimension, on noiseless, continuous, box-bounded problems. Outside that range nothing is
claimed. Results beyond bbob are <Claim id="beyond.bbob" />.

## Does it need gradients?

No. It uses function values only. Some stages (SLSQP, L-BFGS-B and a BFGS variant) estimate gradients by finite
differences, and those evaluations count against your budget. If you can get gradients cheaply, a gradient-based
method is the better tool.

## Does it work on noisy functions?

Untested, and not supported: the objective must be deterministic. With noise, `result.fun` is the best value
observed, which is biased low. See [wrapping your objective](/guides/#wrapping-your-objective).

## Why did it lose on my function or in cell X?

The cells where AMM is credibly behind a competitor in the final test are listed on the [limits](/limits/) page with
the leading competitor named. The most frequent leaders are restarted SLSQP, mostly at 10 to 30 evaluations per
dimension on smooth functions, and HCMA's archive levels, among others on f7 and f19. The cells where AMM is behind the best
competitor by more than 0.02 in the in-sample comparison are drawn on the [results](/results/) heatmap and listed on the
same page. In that in-sample comparison, SLSQP is the best competitor in <Claim id="field.behind_slsqp" /> of the
<Claim id="field.behind" /> behind cells, and <Claim id="field.behind_le30" /> are at 10 or 30 evaluations per
dimension. The functions with the most in-sample behind cells are f8 and f9, then f7. Three caveats apply to the
in-sample list. The margin is a threshold, not a test. A competitor that is missing in a cell (HCMA at dimension 40,
NGOpt at dimension 10, most of TPE) cannot lead it. The cause of a particular loss is not given on this page: the [research
log](/research-log/) records what was tried.

For your own function, the first things to check are in the
[guides](/guides/#when-something-goes-wrong): determinism, the box, and the budget in multiples of the dimension.

## How were the results preregistered?

During development, <Claim id="prereg.cycles" /> cycles are logged. Each candidate change was screened and
then confirmed on instance ids that were not used for the choice, and the ids spent are recorded in a ledger.
The ids <Claim id="ids.dev" /> were used for development, so figures on them are in-sample. The configuration
was frozen on 2026-10-22 (config hash <Claim id="freeze.config_hash" />; the tag <Claim id="freeze.tag" /> is in the research archive, not in the public repository). The final test FT1
on ids <Claim id="ids.final" /> had a written, approved preregistration with predicted ranges and falsifiers before any
run. Its results are in, and every preregistered prediction held: the ten comparisons with IPOP and SLSQP, and the counts of
cells credibly behind (<Claim id="ft1.f2_behind" /> of 600, predicted 30 to 75) and credibly ahead of all
(<Claim id="ft1.f3_ahead_all" />, predicted 70 to 120). The deviations from the plan were logged before any result was
read. The [research log](/research-log/) is where the preregistrations, the deviations and the failed predictions are
kept. The preregistration documents themselves are in the research archive (DOI: <Claim id="archive.doi" />, pending), not in the public code repository.

## Was the final test run more than once?

The runs were made once. The analysis was run twice on them, and the site reports the second (v2). A first analysis (v1)
was published before some of NGOpt's runs had finished, a deviation that was logged before any result was read. v2 is the
same preregistered script run after all arms had finished, with NGOpt at every dimension. The comparisons with IPOP and
SLSQP do not involve NGOpt and are the same in both; the counts that involve NGOpt differ. The first analysis stays in
the [research log](/research-log/#the-final-test-ft1) with its numbers.

## Can I trust the benchmark?

Read it with these limits in mind:

- It is one suite, bbob. The final test has one run per cell and instance; the comparisons with IPOP and SLSQP carry
  bootstrap intervals, and the cell counts are counts, not significance statements. The in-sample numbers have one seed
  per instance and no intervals.
- The final test is the same 24 functions as the development, on new instances.
- The in-sample `c46` series is a composite of two runs (see [Reproduce](/reproduce/)).
- Competitors were run by the author, not by their developers. HCMA is archive data on other instances. The
  Optuna and Nevergrad versions and the hardware were not recorded.
- Coverage of the competitors is unequal across cells.

What helps: every figure states its conditions and has a reproduce block, losing cells are shown on the same
grid as winning ones, and the code and the configuration hash are public. The data download (the research archive) is pending.
